
# Company Brain Interactive Understanding System
## Master Plan v3.0
### From Documents → Mental Model → Understanding

---

# Vision

The Company Brain architecture is described across six canonical documents.
Nobody should have to read them to understand it.

This system is the Company Brain's first act of self-demonstration.
It takes what the Brain does for organizational knowledge —
making it navigable, relational, contextual, and understandable —
and applies it to the Company Brain's own architecture.

The experience should feel less like reading documentation
and more like having a knowledgeable colleague walk you through
exactly how something works, adjusting their explanation
to who you are and what you need to understand.

---

# What This Is Not

- Not a document browser
- Not a technical registry
- Not a features list
- Not a slideshow

---

# What This Is

A system that builds the Company Brain mental model
progressively, visually, and relationally —
starting from the problem it solves
and ending with complete architectural understanding.

The experience itself demonstrates what the Brain is designed to do:
take something complex and make it understood.

---

# Core Design Principles

## 1. Problem First, Architecture Second

Never introduce architecture before the problem it solves.

Wrong order:
> "The Memory Layer consists of five memory types..."

Right order:
> "Organizations forget constantly. Here's what they forget.
> Here's what that costs. Here's how the Brain solves it."

Then introduce memory types.

## 2. One Sentence First

Every concept must be explainable in one sentence
before any further explanation is offered.

That sentence answers:
> "What is this, in plain language?"

Only then reveal depth.

## 3. "So What?" Before "What"

Before explaining what something is,
answer why anyone should care.

Every concept should answer:
> "What breaks if this doesn't exist?"

This is the fastest path to understanding.

## 4. Mental Models Before Terminology

Teach the concept before introducing the name.

Wrong:
> "Knowledge Objects are canonical representations..."

Right:
> "Before the Brain can remember anything,
> it needs a standard way to write things down.
> Think of it like the Brain's filing format.
> That's what Knowledge Objects are."

Technical terms appear as references, not introductions.

## 5. Relationships Over Definitions

People understand systems through connections,
not isolated definitions.

Every concept answers:
- What creates me?
- What do I feed into?
- What breaks without me?
- Where else do I appear?
- Why am I separate from things that seem similar?

## 6. Progressive Disclosure

Four depths available for every concept:


One sentence
↓
One paragraph
↓
Full explainer with visuals and examples
↓
Canonical document reference


Users choose their depth.
The system never forces depth.

## 7. Audience-Adaptive Experience

The same architecture means different things to different people.
The system adapts to who is asking, not to what is documented.

## 8. Visual First

Architecture is spatial.
Spatial things should be explored spatially.
Every major concept has a visual.
Everything clickable.

## 9. The Brain Demonstrates Itself

This system should behave the way the Brain behaves:
surfacing the right explanation at the right moment,
connecting concepts relationally,
answering "why" before "what",
and making complex things understood.

# Section 4
# The Reality Pipeline
## The Single Most Important Explainer

**Purpose:** show how one real event becomes memory, intelligence, and action.

**Format:** animated step-by-step walkthrough.

**This is the centrepiece of the entire system.**

The walkthrough follows a single event — a customer meeting — through every layer.


Step 1: The Meeting Happens (Reality)
A sales call with Acme Corp.
Maria, Sarah, and the client's VP are on the call.
Three things are discussed: a delayed proposal,
a new requirement, and a verbal commitment to deliver by Q3.

Step 2: Capture
The Brain observes the meeting.
It ingests the transcript, the calendar event,
and the CRM notes. Raw data.

Step 3: Understanding
The Brain breaks the meeting into 10 atomic building blocks.
→ Actors: Maria, Sarah, VP of Engineering, Acme Corp
→ Communication: the discussion itself
→ Commitment: "deliver updated proposal by Q3"
→ Goal: Q3 renewal target
→ Resource: the contract
→ Time: Q3 deadline
→ Relationship: Maria manages Sarah; Acme is a customer

Step 4: Knowledge Objects
The Brain creates structured representations.
→ A Person Object for each participant
→ A Meeting Object for the call
→ A Commitment Object for the Q3 promise
→ A Customer Object for Acme Corp
These are the Brain's standardized "filing format."

Step 5: Memory Formation
The objects go into the right memory stores.
→ Who attended: Factual Memory
→ Why the discussion happened: Interaction Memory
→ Q3 commitment: Commitment Memory
→ What was actually decided: Action Memory

Step 6: Intelligence
The Brain reasons over the memory.
→ Detects: this commitment is 30 days old with no progress
→ Detects: Sarah hasn't engaged Acme in 3 weeks
→ Generates: Recommendation ("Schedule Acme check-in")
→ Generates: Operational Risk ("Q3 renewal at risk")

Step 7: Exposure
Maria opens Gmail to write to the Acme VP.
The Brain surfaces, inside Gmail:
the risk flag, the unfulfilled commitment,
Sarah's last contact date, and a suggested action.
Maria didn't search for any of this.
The Brain delivered it at the moment it was needed.

Step 8: Evolution
Maria accepts the recommendation.
The Brain logs: recommendation accepted, action taken.
If this same pattern occurs across three other accounts,
the Brain learns a new rule:
"Commitments with no progress after 30 days need a prompt."


Every step is visually animated.
Each step can be paused and explored.
Technical terms appear on hover, not inline.

# Section 6
# The Vocabulary Layer
## Ontology & Knowledge Objects

**Purpose:** explain the bridge between "understanding reality" and "storing memory."

**This is the hardest concept to explain.
It must be handled with care.**

**Opens with the problem it solves:**
> The Brain has extracted the ten building blocks from a meeting.
> But "Actor" and "Commitment" are too abstract to store.
> The Brain needs a richer vocabulary.
> It needs to know the difference between a Person, a Team,
> a Project, a Meeting, a Decision, an Agreement, and a Policy.
> That vocabulary is the Ontology.

**Ontology explained simply:**
> Think of it as the Brain's dictionary of organizational life.
> 60 defined "things" your organization has:
> People, Teams, Projects, Meetings, Decisions, Agreements,
> Policies, Workflows, Customers, Vendors, Incidents...
> Each one has a precise definition and relationships to others.

**Knowledge Objects explained — the hardest part:**
> Once the Brain understands something using the Ontology vocabulary,
> it creates a standardized representation of it.
> This is a Knowledge Object.
> It's the Brain's "canonical filing card" for that thing.
>
> A Knowledge Object is NOT memory.
> It's the format memory is written in.
>
> Think of it like this:
> Before a librarian can file a book,
> they fill in a card: title, author, subject, date.
> That card is the Knowledge Object.
> The filing system is Memory.

**Visual:** show a meeting transforming into typed Knowledge Objects,
then those objects flowing into different memory stores.

**Why this matters (for technical audiences):**
> Knowledge Objects are human-readable, AI-readable,
> traceable, versionable, and portable.
> They are the canonical representation layer
> that makes the Brain interoperable — not just internally,
> but eventually with other systems and organizations.

# Section 7
# Five Ways of Remembering
## Memory Layer

**Opens with the question everyone asks:**
> Why five memory stores?
> Why not just one?

**Answer, simply:**
> Because "what exists" and "why a decision was made"
> are fundamentally different kinds of knowledge.
> They decay differently.
> They're queried differently.
> They require different governance.
> If you put them in the same store,
> they corrupt each other.

**Visual:** five distinct stores, each with:
- A plain-language name
- The question it answers
- A real example
- Technical name on hover


The Directory       → What exists?
(Factual Memory)
"Who works here? What teams exist? What are our goals?
What policies govern us?"
Example: Sarah Chen, Senior AE, reports to Maria, owns Acme account.

The Reasoning Store → Why was it decided?
(Interaction Memory)
"Why did we choose Vendor A? What alternatives were considered?
Who objected? What was the deciding factor?"
Example: We chose Vendor A despite higher cost because of the SLA.

The Promise Store   → Who owes what to whom?
(Commitment Memory)
"What has been promised? What is overdue?
Who is responsible for what?"
Example: "Deliver updated proposal to Acme by Q3" — status: Promised.

The Action Store    → What actually happened?
(Action Memory)
"What was executed? What was the outcome?
How does this process actually run in practice?"
Example: Maria sent the follow-up email at 2:14pm. Client responded 3 hours later.

The Lessons Store   → What was learned?
(Learning Memory)
"What patterns have emerged? What mistakes were made?
What should never happen again? What consistently works?"
Example: Accounts with 30-day engagement gaps have a 3x higher churn risk.


**The provenance principle:**
> Every record in every store carries:
> who said it, when, from what source, and how confident.
> Every fact knows where it came from.
> Nothing is anonymous. Nothing can be silently changed.

# Section 8
# How the Brain Thinks
## Intelligence Layer

**Opens with the distinction:**
> Memory stores things.
> Intelligence understands them.
>
> The Intelligence layer reads across all five memory stores,
> connects the dots, detects patterns,
> and generates ten types of outputs
> that humans and AI systems can act on.

**Visual:** ten intelligence output types, each with:
- Plain-language name
- One-sentence description
- Real example
- When it's generated


Recommendation      → "You should do this."
                      Connects memory patterns to suggested actions.

Operational Risk    → "Something might go wrong."
                      Detects threats to goals, commitments, resources.
                      Never suppressed, even at low confidence.

Opportunity Signal  → "You could gain something here."
                      Identifies unrealized potential.

Drift Signal        → "What's happening doesn't match what's supposed to happen."
                      Detects gap between policy and practice.

Knowledge Gap       → "We don't know something we should know."
                      Identifies missing organizational understanding.

Prediction          → "Here's what's likely to happen."
                      Forecast based on patterns in memory.

Policy Violation    → "Someone did something they shouldn't have."
                      Hard rule breach detected.

Process Bottleneck  → "This step is consistently slowing everything down."
                      Execution pattern analysis.

Agent Readiness     → "This AI agent has (or hasn't) what it needs to act."
                      Authorization check before autonomous action.

Brain Score         → "Here's the health of this team / project / organization."
                      Aggregate indicator rolled up from all the above.


**The confidence principle:**
> Every intelligence output has a confidence level.
> The Brain never hides uncertainty.
> A low-confidence risk is surfaced with a flag —
> it is better to flag an unconfirmed risk
> than to miss a real one.

# Section 9
# Who Keeps It Honest
## Trust & Governance

**Opens with the differentiation:**
> Most AI memory systems stop at Intelligence.
> Company Brain adds a layer that most AI systems skip entirely:
> Trust.
>
> Intelligence without Trust is just suggestions.
> Memory without Trust is just data.
> Trust is what makes the Brain an organizational system
> rather than a tool.

**The six trust objects, simply:**


Claim       → Every memory record and intelligence output
              is a claim. It carries a trust lifecycle:
              Asserted → Verified → Trusted → (if disputed) Challenged → Revalidated.

Evidence    → Every claim is backed by evidence:
              the source, the context, the authority behind it.

Challenge   → Any claim can be formally disputed.
              A challenge is not a deletion request.
              It is a request for review.
              Both versions persist until resolved.

Approval    → Some actions require authorization
              before they can be executed.
              Who approves what follows the Authority Model.

Exception   → Sometimes a policy is intentionally broken.
              Exceptions are not hidden — they are approved,
              time-limited, and auditable.

Delegation  → Authority can be transferred.
              But delegation cannot create authority that doesn't exist.
              You can only give what you have.


**The Trust Failure Principle:**
> When the Brain cannot satisfy trust requirements,
> it does not proceed with reduced confidence.
> It reduces automation, surfaces the uncertainty,
> and escalates to a human.
> Trust always overrides autonomy.

**Why this is the differentiator:**
> Organizations don't run on intelligence.
> They run on authority, accountability, responsibility, and traceability.
> Every other AI memory product ignores this.
> Company Brain is built on it.

# Section 11
# The Learning Loop
## Evolution Layer

**Simply:**
> The Brain does not just remember.
> It improves.
>
> Every time a Recommendation is accepted or rejected,
> every time a Risk resolves or escalates,
> every time an Agent succeeds or fails —
> the Brain records the outcome.
>
> When a pattern repeats across multiple independent instances,
> the Brain proposes a new learning:
> a new lesson, a refined rule, an updated process.
>
> This proposal does not write itself into memory.
> It is reviewed and approved through the same
> Write Governance that governs all memory.
> The Brain improves with human oversight, not around it.

**The one rule:**
> Single feedback ≠ Learning.
> Repeated, validated feedback = Learning.
> The Brain earns its improvements.

# Anti-Patterns to Avoid

**Never:**
- Lead with technical terminology
- Show a list of 177 objects on the home screen
- Present architecture layers as navigation tabs
- Use phrases like "canonical Knowledge Object" without first explaining Knowledge Objects
- Show a feature before explaining the problem it solves
- Build pages that feel like documentation sections
- Make any concept a dead end (every concept must link to something)
- Present the six documents as the entry point
- Introduce Ontology before explaining why the Brain needs a vocabulary

**Always:**
- Problem before solution
- Plain language before technical terms
- One sentence before a paragraph
- Visual before explanation
- "Why does this exist?" before "What is it?"
- Every concept links to related concepts
- Every concept links to a real scenario where it appears
